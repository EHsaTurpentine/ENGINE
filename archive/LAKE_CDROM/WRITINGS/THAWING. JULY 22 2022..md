I’d lost complete track of time and reality by the time I’m coloring pages in LAKE and teaching myself not just how to hold a pencil and move it again but to try to keep doing it no matter how pointless and fruitless it was. It’s significant these begin after I’ve already proven to myself I’d live long enough to get the fuck out of that hospital. I didn’t sleep the entire time I was in there, almost four full weeks. I was beyond delirious when I fell asleep for the first time back in Gresham; they’d let me go earlier than they should have because I was coming more and more unraveled the longer I stayed on the 4th floor and the days didn’t distinguish themselves anymore. 

Which happened almost instantly; I think it was like Thursday of that first full week when I started considering the rise and fall of the sun as marking what others were living as lives, literally one day at a time. 

Every day T would come to the hospital as early as she could and every afternoon she would fall asleep in the chair and I would tell her she needed to rest and to go home and when she did that’s when the hardest parts were by a lot. 

When I look at this first one, it still feels like I’m stuck in that first room just opposite the elevator. The room where I watched the walls run and shit-talked myself right into a Valium nap at like 3 am one of the first full nights I was on the floor and badgering the nurses to just talk to me and distract me from the night terrors that would set in like gravity right around nightfall, when my hypervigilance goes into overdrive and always has because I quite really taught myself how to see in the dark. 

How to imagine the worst things possible occupying every square millimeter of any space I’m trapped in at any given time, and then steel myself for the very worst possibilities given the circumstances, at least as far as I can gather in any given moment. 

This is why being in the hospital broke my brain and darkened my spirit so quickly; as soon as Dr. Z told me he was going to “TRY” and save my life, I couldn’t unhear that one simple word, those three simple letters, that first word of the three word slogan we’d all adapted to survive the pandemic: 

TRY. FAIL. LEARN. 

In the hospital, there was zero more room for error and I knew it. I didn’t ever realize how close I’d come to dying. I still haven’t. My brain shattered in the realization that it still depends on this busted body, and unleashed my deepest self-hatreds and self-resentments. I fought with myself every second I was in there in ways I had never fought myself before. I found strengths I didn’t know I had but they were quickly deflated by weaknesses I didn’t know I had, either; the debilitation I felt from the bag that every single second saved my fucking life was beyond total; when I got home to the full length mirror in the same bathroom where I almost and very much should have died, I couldn’t and wouldn’t believe anything that I ever saw and it took months for me to even look myself in the face in the mirror when I brushed my teeth. I stopped exaggerating entirely and talking mostly. I didn’t eat any substantial for the better part of two years. Parts of me died in the hospital that needed to die, be cut out, etc. 

The rest of me, though, lived.

When I took at this first one, I can still feel how slow my thoughts and responses arrived to any and everything around me. I couldn’t believe for a single second what was really happening for almost two full years. And still. I’ll never understand it and I’ll never get over it. When Matthew Perry died, I was heartbroken. But also jealous. Getting reversal surgery doesn’t take any of that stuff away, either. No, I don’t wear the bag anymore and I’m fucking lucky as fuck I got to wear one for a year in the first place. That’s the reasonable part of me that’s still left of me to be reasonable. For weeks upon months I stewed and cussed my own existence. I never completely learned how to stop doing that kind of constant self-loathing; there will always be a part of me that never leaves the hospital, because I know I’ll be lucky to be in one when I die. 

Not if, either.

I’d been home and back already. T was terrified my blood pressure wouldn’t stabilize and the drains were still pulling years of infection out of my almost-dead whatever. Husk isn’t the right word for how it felt because there isn’t a word for how it felt. People who’ve had near death experiences talk about an absence of feeling that displaces the constant anxiety we have because we are so very conscious that we are going to die and will largely do anything within and even beyond our powers to delay the inevitable. When I agreed to go back in the hospital, I knew it wasn’t really a choice. They’d sent me home the first time because I couldn’t and wouldn’t sleep; quite really, I wouldn’t and couldn’t until I was back in the spaces where I shouldn’t have taken further steps, breathes, drags, etc. etc. Proof of concept, I guess. I’d pretty much given up on the idea of ever sleeping ever again by the end of the first week anyway; as the nights stacked up, the nursing staff tolerated more and more of my evening outbursts. You cannot pay the people who do this work enough. Not in money, not in respect, not in benefits, not in any way. The people who worked in the hospital that summer constantly talked and calmed me down in ways that nothing else has ever been able to; the emergent justification for all those IV drugs sprouted that PICC line in my arm that I very much considered a blessing and miracle and still very much do. 

Still, the absence in this first one is hard to deal with. All that white space. Not so much quiet or still as just empty. That’s how I felt every single second. It wasn’t the first time I’d ever felt that way, either. Not by a lot. I can’t remember the first time I felt it, an emptiness that is beyond the capacity of the word. Cliche and cop out, yeah. That doesn’t change the reality of it feeling exactly like that, though. An empty beyond empty. 

It’s significant there’s a whole word I’ve completed in this one. The word is significant, too. The majority of my life has been encased in ice, frozen in some way. This isn’t unique to me but I don’t have another frame of reference; if I did, I’m sure it would be like picking up someone else’s eyeglasses or pushing someone else’s contact lenses against my eyeballs an expecting to see everything another human being sees. 

I’d been proving Dr. Z and everyone right for 11 days straight when I started coloring this. I remember being particularly delirious the morning of July 11 when they handed me a little tray with a tube of yogurt and a banana under a heavy domed lid made of that antibacterial plastic stuff, that sterilite or whatever it’s called because every fucking thing in the hospital is made out of it, everything every where plastic and plastic and plastic with just splashes of stainless and surgical steels; the illusion that everything in your room is sterile evaporated within hours of that first full day; they didn’t “deep” clean the room until after they’d moved me across and then further down the hall, further away from the elevator and nursing station.  That was a long, long way away from July 22 still. I didn’t know what to think when they brought the tray other than I was completely terrified to put solid food back into my body again; I had already tried to bargain with Dr. Z within the first 48 hours of being there: I will eat/drink/not eat/not drink any fucking thing you tell me for the rest of my life if I can just go home and try to forget as much of this as possible pretty pretty pretty please I will do anything give anything say anything just tell me what it is and I will do it; he just laughed at me and told me I needed to calm down.

It took me two days to “finish” this one. Which really means, I started out like I’d always ended up: good intentions at best, wasting time at worst. The hospital showed me how fluid time really is, once you strip everything else away and just feel the seconds tick by, hoping each one is better than the last. Which of course it’s not. We are all of us incredible and odds defying miracles; we don’t ever feel like we are, but though, which is what gives our lives real edge: to begrudge your own miracle is to practice a particularly shitty kind of self-righteousness that is tough to beat.
