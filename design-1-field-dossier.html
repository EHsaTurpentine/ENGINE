Field Map — Thomas Fork to Three Island Crossing
USGS 7½′ quadrangle, overlaid with emigrant route data, BLM field survey Map 1 of 121

After reaching the plain, the course of the stream is marked by a line of green timber, which gave rise to its name among the early trappers — “Boisse,” or the “Wooded River.” This green strip of vegetation, winding its way through the desert sage plain, gave a more cheerful prospect to the view.

So wrote Winfield S. Ebey on August 20, 1854, somewhere along a corridor that four decades of wagon traffic had already begun to carve into the Snake River Plain. Between 1841 and roughly 1869, an estimated route system of more than 120 surveyed segments carried Oregon-bound settlers, California gold-seekers, missionaries, army expeditions, and freight wagons across what is now southern Idaho — along the primary Oregon Trail, its South Alternate, the California Trail, the Salt Lake Alternate, Hudspeth’s Cutoff, the Lander Road, and Goodale’s Cutoff.

This dossier reproduces the working structure of that 1993 field survey: eight route segments, each mapped, walked, and marked. What follows is a reader’s version of that record.

Route Index

Marked on public land with white carsonite posts, BLM
01
Emigrants entering present-day Idaho from Thomas Fork still had the greater part of their journey ahead. The primary route runs southwest through Fort Hall, then follows the Snake River Plain toward Three Island Crossing, the trail’s most consequential river ford.
02
At Three Island Crossing the trail fords the Snake and proceeds northwest toward the Boise River. Some emigrants instead crossed further upstream, near Thousand Springs, avoiding the ford entirely.
03
For emigrants who chose not to ford at Three Island, this alternate held to the south bank of the Snake, a longer but drier passage toward the Oregon line.
04
California-bound and Oregon-bound emigrants shared a common road as far as the Raft River Crossing. There, the California-bound turned southwest through Granite Pass — a route Joseph B. Chiles is believed to have first located in 1842.
05
Entering Idaho just west of Snowville, Utah, this longer alternate rejoined the California Trail at City of Rocks. Forty-niners anxious to reach the gold fields largely avoided it in favor of faster routes.
06
Opened in 1849, this cutoff let California-bound emigrants leave the Oregon Trail earlier, at Soda Springs, rather than continuing on to Raft River — shaving days from an already urgent trip.
07
Congress funded this 1857 wagon road as a southern alternative to the central emigrant corridor — the only federally engineered emigrant road built during the trail era.
08
Tim Goodale led a wagon train of nearly 800 men, 300 women and children north of the Snake River in 1862 — a route large and well-armed enough to avoid the misfortune that met smaller, more exposed trains that year.
Field note — Smith’s Trading Post marker, BLM 1989

In 1848, Thomas L. “Peg Leg” Smith set up a trading post on the Bear River, near present-day Dingle, to sell supplies to passing emigrants. The post itself lasted only a couple of seasons. What remains is this Idaho Transportation Department historical marker along U.S. Highway 30 — a reminder that most of the trail’s infrastructure was as improvised and short-lived as the migration itself.

Trail ethics

Historic and prehistoric sites and artifacts on public land are protected under the Archaeological Resources Protection Act of 1979. Nothing may be removed or disturbed — what is found in place should be left in place, so the next visitor has the same chance to see it.
